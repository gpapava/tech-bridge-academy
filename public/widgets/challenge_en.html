A Challenge is a teaching strategy in which students are called to face an authentic problem — proposed by the school or, preferably, by a company — and elaborate the solution they consider most effective within a defined time. Its design derives from Problem Based Learning (PBL): an approach in which learning develops through processes of investigation, research, and reflection activated by the need to understand and resolve complex problems.
Authentic problem
Students face a real, open-ended problem with no single correct answer — proposed by a company from its actual operational context.
Investigation process
Students must understand the problem, collect information, formulate hypotheses, compare alternative solutions, motivate their choices, and present their work publicly.
Competitive dimension
Participants — individually or in groups — present their proposal to a panel of judges. Competition increases engagement and stimulates critical comparison between different approaches.
Open-ended solution
The final solution can take very different forms: a prototype, a procedure, an organisational process, an algorithm, a simulation, an improvement proposal. What characterises the Challenge is the cognitive process, not the type of final product.
Defined timeframe
Participation modes, timelines, and final evaluation criteria are made explicit at the outset. The challenge can run over several weeks, giving students adequate time for analysis and solution-building.
Scalable company involvement
Minimum: design, launch, and final evaluation. Maximum: design and launch + company labs and equipment + evaluation and award ceremony.
A Challenge can be individual or group-based. Both modalities have specific advantages and can be chosen according to the learning objectives pursued. A special intensive format — the Hackathon — exists for contexts where time pressure is itself a learning variable.
Individual

The main objective is to assess personal performance or highly specialised technical and professional competences. Used in professional competitions, programming contests, CAD design competitions, or technical competitions where the result depends primarily on the individual's performance.

Group

Develops not only technical and professional competences but also transversal ones — especially the ability to work in a team. The solution is the result of a collective knowledge-building process. Groups of four to six students with different competences and personal characteristics are recommended. The most widely used format in school-business collaboration pathways.

Hackathon

An intensive event in which teams work on solving the problem within a very limited time (for example, 24 or 48 hours), characterised by particularly intense work rhythms and strong time pressure. The Challenge, by contrast, is a broader and more flexible device that can run over several weeks, leaving students adequate time for problem analysis, information research, and solution construction.

The Challenge is particularly effective in developing both technical and professional competences and transversal competences. Students do not simply apply already-known procedures, but learn to select and integrate different knowledge to face new situations.

Project Work and Challenge share many elements: both are grounded in authentic situations, require the integrated mobilisation of technical and transversal competences, and promote active learning in which students take a leading role. The difference lies not in the final result but in the educational logic that guides the learning process.

Like all work-based learning devices, the Challenge is structured in five phases. During the delivery phase, students follow an internal process articulated in four steps — from problem understanding to final presentation.
The quality of a Challenge depends largely on collaboration between company, school, and students. Each party plays a specific and complementary role.
School / VET centre
  • Prepare students for the Challenge
  • Organise work groups
  • Facilitate the learning process
  • Support teams throughout the pathway
  • Coordinate the assessment process together with the company
Company
  • Identify an authentic situation on which to build the Challenge
  • Collaborate with the school in defining learning objectives
  • Make data, information, and technical documentation available
  • Accompany the groups through the company tutor
  • Participate in the final assessment
  • Assign a recognition or award to the best solutions (optional)
Students
  • Understand the problem
  • Collect information and formulate hypotheses
  • Analyse data and make shared decisions
  • Document the work carried out
  • Present and argue the elaborated solution
  • Participate responsibly — the effectiveness of the Challenge depends on the full engagement of every team member
Assessment should not be limited to the quality of the final solution. One of the main strengths of the Challenge is the possibility of observing the entire process through which students face a complex problem. For this reason, assessment should consider three complementary dimensions simultaneously.
Quality of the analysis process
Capacity to understand the problem and identify its essential elements; formulate pertinent questions; collect and use reliable information; formulate and verify hypotheses; critically compare possible alternatives; use a coherent working method.
Quality of the proposed solution
Coherence with the initial problem; effectiveness of the proposal; technical and organisational feasibility; degree of innovation; sustainability of the solution; quality of the documentation produced. In most Challenges there is no single correct answer — assessment recognises the quality of the proposal and the solidity of the motivations supporting it.
Quality of team collaboration
Active participation of all members; mutual listening; balanced task distribution; integration of different competences; constructive conflict management; assumption of responsibility; capacity to make shared decisions. Collaboration is not merely an organisational tool — it is itself an important learning objective.
The preferred assessment tool is the evaluation rubric, which makes observation criteria and expected proficiency levels explicit. It allows school and company to share a common language of assessment and integrates all evidence collected during the activity: teacher and company tutor observations, products made, technical documentation, final presentation, and student self-assessment.
Three examples of Challenges in the mechanical and mechatronic sector. Each example shows the problem, what students are asked to do, and why it qualifies as a Challenge rather than a Project Work.
Example 1 · Manufacturing efficiency
Reduce format changeover times on an automated line
A mechatronic company finds that format changeover on an automated line takes excessive time, resulting in productivity losses. Teams must analyse the production process, identify the main causes of inefficiencies, and propose a solution that reduces changeover times without compromising quality and safety.
Why it is a Challenge: students are not asked to design a new system or build a prototype, but to understand a real problem, collect information, compare different hypotheses, and identify the most convincing solution.
Example 2 · Technical diagnostics
Diagnose a functioning anomaly in an automatic machine
During the final testing of an automatic machine, the production cycle stops in an apparently random way. The company has not yet been able to identify the cause of the malfunction with certainty. Teams must collect information, consult technical documentation, analyse sensors and actuators, formulate different hypotheses, and propose the most convincing solution, motivating it on the basis of the evidence collected.
Why it is a Challenge: the problem has no already-known solution and requires a genuine process of technical investigation.
Example 3 · Ergonomics and production
Improve an assembly workstation
Operators in an assembly department report fatigue and frequent errors during some repetitive operations. Teams must observe the process, analyse the ergonomic, organisational, and productive aspects, compare possible interventions, and propose a solution that simultaneously improves operator wellbeing, work quality, and productivity.
Why it is a Challenge: the problem involves technical, organisational, and ergonomic aspects and requires integrating different competences to find a balanced and sustainable solution.
