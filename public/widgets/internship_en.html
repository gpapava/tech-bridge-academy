Bridge Area · Educational Collaboration · Section 2
A curricular internship is a formative experience that a student carries out at a host company during their period of study, with the aim of facilitating future professional choices through direct knowledge of the world of work and the connection between theory and professional practice. The intern is supervised by a company tutor who acts as facilitator, coach, and assessor. Duration is variable and is generally determined by the study programme and applicable national or regional regulations.

As a general guideline, approximately 60% of internship responsibilities should be devoted to professional development; 40% to paraprofessional tasks or daily operational routine.
Mutual agreement
The intern undertakes work that contributes to the host organisation's objectives; the company offers the opportunity to acquire new competences and explore potential career paths.
Supervised learning
Tasks assigned to the intern must be consistent with their competences and learning objectives, and aligned with the organisation's needs. A company tutor accompanies the entire experience.
Structured learning plan
School and company co-design a formal learning plan with SMART objectives — Specific, Measurable, Relevant, Time-bound — before the intern arrives.
Dual learning function
The tools used serve two purposes: helping the student transform experience into learning, and generating systematic documentation that school and company can use to improve future placements.
Variable duration
Duration is generally determined by the study programme and applicable national or regional regulations. The specific regulatory framework varies by country.
Organisational learning
Each internship contributes not only to the student's learning but also to the organisational learning of the partners involved, feeding a continuous process of improvement of educational partnerships.
The internship allows students to apply and integrate the knowledge and competences acquired at school in real workplace situations. It develops both technical and professional competences specific to the sector, and transversal competences that are increasingly essential in all professional contexts.

Like all work-based learning devices, the curricular internship is structured in five phases. Each phase involves different combinations of school, company, and intern — with specific tools to document and improve the experience.
Three parties are involved in the curricular internship, each with a specific and complementary role. The quality of the experience depends on all three playing their part actively and in coordination.
School / VET centre
  • Identify the school tutor
  • Carry out matching between students and companies
  • Present the company and the training project to the intern
  • Co-design the individual training plan with the company
  • Maintain regular contact with the company and the intern throughout
  • Organise an assessment meeting with the intern at the end
  • Compile the school-company self-assessment report
  • Organise a joint assessment session with the company
Company
  • Identify the company tutor (and optionally a mentor)
  • Define placement conditions: period, hours, any benefits
  • Plan monitoring and assessment meetings
  • Allocate material and immaterial resources for the intern
  • Inform colleagues of the intern's arrival
  • Carry out onboarding activities on day one
  • Accompany the intern through assigned tasks and ensure safety compliance
  • Create an inclusive and welcoming work environment
  • Organise a debrief session at the end and issue a formal completion certificate
Intern (student)
  • Engage actively in assigned tasks and projects
  • Take responsibility for personal and professional growth
  • Demonstrate professionalism and adherence to organisational guidelines
  • Maintain open communication with the supervisor
  • Respect project deadlines and manage time effectively
  • Maintain confidentiality of sensitive information
  • Compile the learning log daily, enriching it with documentation of learning
  • Participate in monitoring and assessment meetings
The internship experience can be genuinely formative — or it can fail to produce real learning. The difference lies entirely in how it is designed and managed. These conditions, drawn from evidence on effective internship programmes, distinguish a quality placement from one that wastes the student's time.
A good internship…
Assigns significant projects and tasks that contribute to the organisation's objectives and provide a genuine learning experience
Provides support, feedback, and growth opportunities from experienced professionals
Allows the intern to develop and improve competences specific to their sector of interest
Encourages the intern to build professional networks within the organisation and the sector
Gives the intern the opportunity to understand and experience the organisation's values, work culture, and professional ethics
Plans regular feedback and assessment sessions to monitor progress, identify areas for improvement, and recognise achievements
Encourages the intern to reflect on their learning experiences and identify lessons to apply to their future professional development
Treats interns fairly and respectfully, with equal access to learning opportunities, resources, and a safe working environment
A poor internship…
Assigns the intern exclusively menial tasks or errands unrelated to their learning objectives, or limits them to passive observation
Fails to provide the necessary support or give regular feedback
Excludes interns from professional development opportunities available to other staff
Burdens the intern with an excessive workload that hinders their capacity to learn and participate actively
Subjects the intern to discrimination, harassment, or unfair treatment
Confines the intern to a single department or role without giving them the opportunity to discover different aspects of the organisation
Creates false promises of employment or unrealistic expectations regarding the intern's role and responsibilities
The internship does not automatically produce learning. Activities carried out at the company become genuinely formative when school and company accompany the student in observation, reflection, and the continuous connection between what is experienced in work contexts and what is learned at school.
